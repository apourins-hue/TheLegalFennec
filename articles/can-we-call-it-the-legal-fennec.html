Behind the name / Trademark law

Can We Call It The Legal Fennec?

A domain was available. Similar names already existed. Here is how a small publication confronted its first trademark question, and what the search did, and did not, establish.

By The Legal Fennec Editorial Team  ·  19 October 2026  ·  Approximately 8 min read

Before launching an independent publication on law, technology and society, we faced a legal question of our own: what could we call it? The answer sounded straightforward. We liked fennecs, the small desert foxes with unusually large ears, and wanted a name with some personality. The Legal Fennec felt memorable without pretending to be a law firm. But enjoying a name and being legally comfortable using it are different things.

We began with ordinary web searches, explored the possibility of a broader identity called The Fennec, then checked trademark databases in the United States and Europe. The exercise did not result in formal clearance or registration. It did, however, illustrate how a seemingly simple branding decision becomes a practical lesson in trademark law.

First, a name is not necessarily a trademark

There are several questions that are easy to confuse: is a domain available? Is another company using the same words? Is the name protected as a trademark? And could our use lead people to think our publication is connected to another business?

These questions have different answers. A registrar may offer an unregistered domain even if someone else has earlier rights in a similar sign. Equally, finding a business with the same animal-inspired name does not automatically mean that nobody else may use it. Trademark rights are territorial, can depend on registration or use, and generally protect a sign in connection with particular goods or services, subject to special protections in some circumstances.

A domain search tells you whether an address may be available to register. It does not establish that the name is legally free to use.

We considered buying thefennec.org as the umbrella address for two possible projects: The Legal Fennec, focused on law and technology, and The Fennec Talks, a possible language-learning platform. Both domain names appeared available in a registrar search. That was useful for planning, but it was only the beginning of the legal inquiry.

Next, we looked beyond exact matches

We searched for existing businesses and projects using “Fennec” and related expressions. We then used the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) database to look for records containing the term. Our search produced multiple results, including live, pending, cancelled and abandoned records.

Some were plainly distant from a legal publication, such as marks connected with devices, apparel or other physical goods. Others called for a closer look: examples visible in our results included Fennec Music, Fennec the Fox for children's books, and Fennec's Place for entertainment activities. A search result alone could not tell us whether any one of them was a legal obstacle. We would need to inspect the actual scope of protection, relevant territory and applicable status.

This distinction is essential. A cancelled application is not the same as a live registration, but a historical record can still be useful when investigating earlier commercial activity. And a mark need not look exactly like yours to raise concerns: sound, appearance, meaning and the commercial impression can matter.

What do trademark classes actually tell us?

Trademark databases organize goods and services using the Nice Classification. Our legal and educational interests led us to inspect a few categories in particular:

ClassWhy we checked it
41Education, training, publication of texts, and cultural activities; especially relevant for a journal or language-learning platform.
45Legal services; potentially relevant if a project later moves beyond publishing general information into professional services.
42Technology and software-related services; potentially relevant if the platform develops or offers technical services.
9Possible downloadable digital publications and software products, depending on their description.
16Printed publications and educational materials.

Classes are a useful index, not a legal verdict. Two marks in Class 41 do not automatically conflict; two marks in different classes can still involve related goods or services. The precise wording of a registration matters more than the number alone.

Then we searched European records

Because this publication is being developed with a European audience in mind, we also consulted TMview. Searching for “Fennec” returned a substantial set of international records. Among the first results we examined were an EU mark associated with X-Technology Swiss GmbH, registered in Classes 23, 25 and 28, and another associated with Airbus Helicopters in Class 28. Neither was an obvious direct match for independent legal publishing based on the classes shown.

But the screenshot covered only a small portion of the results. We did not complete a systematic review of every relevant national register, similarity variant or earlier unregistered business name. It would therefore be inaccurate to say that our name had received legal clearance.

The real question is whether people could be confused

Under European trademark law, likelihood of confusion is evaluated globally. Among the relevant considerations are similarity of the signs, similarity of the goods and services, the intended public, and the distinctiveness of earlier rights. The Court of Justice's Canon judgment is an important reference for assessing whether goods and services are related; EUIPO guidelines develop the broader approach.

In practical terms, the question is not simply “Does another Fennec exist?” It is closer to: “Could the relevant audience reasonably think that these services come from the same undertaking, or from economically connected undertakings?”

That question changes the search. A similarly named clothing brand may be less immediately concerning than an educational publisher serving the same audience. Even then, one must compare the specific activities and relevant legal rights rather than rely on intuition.

Why we chose the narrower name

We eventually decided to pursue The Legal Fennec as the website's public identity and acquired thelegalfennec.org. The wording immediately tells readers something about the field we cover. It also allowed us to continue building the publication without establishing a separate parent brand for projects that might not yet exist.

That choice was a branding and risk-management judgment, not a conclusion that the name is immune from challenge. We did not register a trademark, nor did we obtain a professional clearance opinion. We simply made a more focused decision after a preliminary search.

Five lessons from the exercise

  • Search the internet first, but do not stop there. Business names, websites and social accounts help reveal actual market use.
  • Check the territories that matter. A US result and an EU registration may have very different consequences for a Europe-facing publication.
  • Read the goods and services, not only the class. Class numbers are filters, not guarantees of freedom to operate.
  • Distinguish status carefully. Registered, pending, expired and abandoned records should not be treated alike.
  • Separate domain ownership from trademark rights. Buying an address does not grant exclusivity to a brand name.

A legal publication gets its first lesson from its own name

There is something appropriate about a publication devoted to the interaction of law and everyday life discovering that its first legal question was embedded in its own identity. The name on a website may look like a design decision. Behind it are questions of territorial rights, classification, commercial identity and fair competition.

For us, The Legal Fennec remains a small independent editorial project, open to learning and correction. Choosing its name was not the end of a legal inquiry. It was an introduction to why careful inquiry matters.
