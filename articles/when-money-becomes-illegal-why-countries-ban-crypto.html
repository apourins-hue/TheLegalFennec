When Money Becomes Illegal: Why Some Countries Ban Cryptocurrency

Bitcoin can be traded legally in one country and expose its users to legal consequences in another. The technology does not change at the border. The law does.

Written by The Legal Fennec team  ·  21 October 2026  ·  Approximately 11 min read

Imagine owning Bitcoin. You have not stolen it or used it to purchase anything unlawful. You simply bought a digital asset and kept it in a wallet. In some countries, this is perfectly legal. In others, the same conduct may carry legal consequences.

Cryptocurrencies were developed around an idea that challenges traditional finance: value can be transferred without necessarily relying on banks or central authorities. For governments, however, money has never been just a method of payment. It also affects monetary policy, capital movements, financial supervision and public authority.

That tension explains why a technology built for global networks can be subject to dramatically different national rules.

First, what does “illegal cryptocurrency” actually mean?

The word ban often hides several very different legal rules. A state might refuse to recognise Bitcoin as legal tender; forbid its use to pay for goods; prohibit exchanges and other service providers; impose licensing requirements; restrict mining; or make even the acquisition and holding of virtual assets unlawful.

Not being legal tender does not mean that possession is illegal. Legal tender is a legal status attached to money for the discharge of monetary obligations. Many assets can be lawfully owned without having that status. Conversely, an activity can be illegal even when the underlying asset is not expressly prohibited.

Nor should a regulator's warning automatically be mistaken for a newly enacted criminal offence. To understand a country's approach, we must identify the actual instrument, the conduct it regulates and the applicable consequences.

China: a prohibition focused on cryptocurrency financial activity

China has long taken a restrictive stance. Its 2021 multi-agency notice classified cryptocurrency-related business activities—including exchange services, trading intermediation and token issuance and financing—as illegal financial activities. Foreign platforms were also prohibited from illegally offering relevant services to users in mainland China.

In February 2026, Chinese authorities issued a further notice addressing virtual currencies and risks associated with real-world asset tokenisation. It reaffirmed that cryptocurrencies such as Bitcoin, Ether and stablecoins are not legal tender and that specified virtual-currency business activities are prohibited.

The distinction remains important: the ban on financial services and market transactions should not be described as a single, universal criminal offence covering every private instance of possession. The legal consequences depend on the activity and the applicable rules.

China's restrictions express a model of monetary governance in which financial stability, capital controls and the ability to supervise payment activity receive particular weight.

Algeria: an express ban extending to possession

Algeria presents a more direct statutory example. Law No. 25-10 of 24 July 2025 amended legislation on the prevention and combating of money laundering and terrorist financing. Its new Article 6 bis prohibits issuing, buying, selling, using, holding, trading and promoting virtual assets, along with operating exchange platforms. The prohibition also extends to cryptocurrency mining.

This is legally different from simply refusing to accept Bitcoin as a currency. The text reaches the individual who holds a virtual asset, not just the company offering a trading service.

Its placement in anti-money-laundering legislation also raises a question of legal proportionality. If the public objective is to prevent illicit financial flows, must all possession be prohibited? Or could authorisation, identity checks and transaction-monitoring obligations address the risks through less restrictive means?

The answer is not self-evident. Legislatures may evaluate risks differently, but a rigorous analysis must distinguish the public objective from the breadth of the means chosen to achieve it.

Egypt: a statutory restriction built around authorisation

In Egypt, Law No. 194 of 2020 governs the Central Bank and banking sector. Article 206 restricts the issuance, trading and promotion of cryptocurrencies, the creation or operation of trading platforms and related activities without the Central Bank of Egypt's approval.

The formal legal structure matters: it is framed around official approval, rather than a rule stating that authorisation is conceptually impossible. In practice, the Central Bank's March 2023 warning reported that no licences had been granted for cryptocurrency trading activities in the Egyptian market.

The Central Bank has emphasised volatility, fraud, financial crime and the absence of official guarantees. It has also warned of criminal penalties for prohibited activity. Egypt shows how a licensing requirement can operate as a very substantial barrier when no relevant authorisations are issued.

Morocco: unauthorised payments and foreign-exchange controls

Morocco's authorities have repeatedly warned against transactions involving virtual currencies. On 15 October 2026, Bank Al-Maghrib, the Moroccan Capital Market Authority and the Office des Changes issued a renewed joint warning, stating that the use of virtual currencies remains unauthorised and drawing attention to market volatility, consumer risks and possible illicit uses.

Morocco's legal position is closely connected to the regulation of payments and transactions with foreign countries. The Office des Changes has previously explained that relevant cryptocurrency transactions can breach foreign-exchange rules and expose participants to sanctions.

A warning about the use of virtual currencies is nonetheless not a substitute for identifying the specific statutory offence. In particular, restrictions on making payments should not automatically be presented as identical to an express prohibition on every instance of private holding.

Tunisia: when technology meets a much older exchange-control code

Tunisia illustrates a different problem: applying an inherited legal framework to a technology that did not exist when the framework was designed. Its foreign-exchange regime continues to rely substantially on Law No. 76-18 of 21 January 1976, together with implementing rules and Central Bank supervision.

Cryptocurrency activities can raise questions under rules on foreign-exchange transactions, cross-border transfers and unauthorised financial activity. However, Tunisia should not simply be placed in the same category as Algeria, whose law expressly prohibits holding virtual assets.

The Tunisian government discussed a comprehensive reform of the foreign-exchange code in March 2024. A further legislative proposal was under parliamentary examination in 2026, reflecting continued interest in modernising rules designed for an earlier financial era. At the time of this article, we have not verified the enactment of a comprehensive legal framework generally authorising cryptocurrency trading.

The case illustrates why legal uncertainty is itself a regulatory problem. Citizens and businesses need clear answers about what is permitted, what requires authorisation and what conduct may be penalised.

Why do governments prohibit or restrict cryptocurrency?

Monetary sovereignty. Central banks influence national monetary conditions, and governments may seek to preserve the role of domestic currency. Widespread use of privately issued digital assets, especially foreign-currency-linked stablecoins, can complicate monetary policy and capital-flow management.

Money laundering and financial crime. Some digital-asset services can move value across borders rapidly. This creates supervisory challenges, but it would be inaccurate to portray every blockchain transaction as anonymous or criminal. Many public blockchains maintain visible transaction records. The key legal questions involve identifying customers, tracing transactions and assigning compliance responsibilities.

Consumer and investor protection. Price volatility, market manipulation, lost private keys, fraud and exchange failures can cause serious losses. Financial authorities may intervene because protections familiar from bank accounts and regulated investment services do not necessarily apply.

Foreign-exchange controls. A system allowing peer-to-peer transfers across borders may conflict with rules intended to channel international payments through approved intermediaries. That concern is especially salient in jurisdictions with extensive capital restrictions.

These concerns establish potential reasons for intervention. They do not, by themselves, determine whether a blanket ban is the most effective or proportionate legal response.

The European Union: regulation instead of a general ban

The European Union has taken a contrasting approach through the Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation (MiCA), Regulation (EU) 2023/1114. MiCA provides harmonised rules for specified categories of crypto-assets and for the businesses issuing them or providing related services.

Its provisions became applicable in stages, with the principal framework applying from 30 December 2024. Relevant crypto-asset service providers generally need authorisation and must meet governance, conduct, disclosure and safeguarding obligations, subject to the regulation's scope and exemptions.

The EU-wide transitional deadline for previously operating providers expired on 1 July 2026, although some Member States had shorter transitional arrangements. The European Securities and Markets Authority confirmed that providers lacking the required authorisation after the applicable deadline must cease unauthorised services.

MiCA is not a promise that every crypto investment is safe, nor does it cover every form of blockchain activity. It shows, instead, a different legislative choice: managing identified risks through rules for regulated intermediaries rather than declaring cryptocurrency ownership generally unlawful.

Does an outright ban work?

A government can shut down a domestic exchange, penalise unlicensed providers and restrict the banking system's involvement in cryptocurrency payments. It cannot necessarily stop all transfers occurring through decentralised networks outside national borders.

This creates practical questions of enforcement. Activity displaced from regulated businesses may migrate to informal or offshore channels, potentially making supervision and consumer protection harder. Conversely, jurisdictions with limited supervisory capacity may see targeted restrictions as a temporary or necessary response to specific risks.

In a 2023 policy paper, the International Monetary Fund analysed these trade-offs. Its Executive Board generally agreed that strict bans were not the preferred first option, although targeted restrictions might be appropriate depending on a country's policy objectives and institutional capacity. A few directors did not rule out outright bans.

That is not a universal legal verdict. It is a reminder that public authorities must evaluate enforceability, proportionality and the unintended consequences of restrictions rather than assuming that a prohibition automatically removes the underlying activity.

The deeper legal question

Cryptocurrency regulation is ultimately a debate about the reach of state authority in a borderless financial environment. For any restriction, lawyers should ask: what public objective is being pursued? Is the prohibited conduct defined clearly enough? Are the sanctions proportionate? Does the measure distinguish between risks arising from private holding, payments, exchanges and professional financial services?

These questions do not produce the same answer in every legal system. They do reveal why slogans about countries “banning Bitcoin” are too imprecise to substitute for legal analysis.

Conclusion: the blockchain has no borders, but the law does

In Algeria, legislation expressly reaches the holding of virtual assets. In China, authorities prohibit a wide range of cryptocurrency financial activities. Egypt uses a restrictive prior-approval model. Morocco maintains prohibitions and warnings relating to the use of virtual currencies, while Tunisia continues to grapple with older foreign-exchange laws. The European Union has opted to authorise and supervise substantial parts of the crypto-asset market.

A decentralised technology does not operate outside the law. But neither does declaring an activity illegal make the technology disappear. The central challenge is to develop rules that protect financial stability and individuals while remaining clear, proportionate and capable of being enforced.

Should governments prevent people from using cryptocurrencies, or establish the conditions under which they can use them safely?
