You Created It. But Do You Own It?

Copyright celebrates authorship. Yet in many creative industries, the person who makes the work is not the person who controls its commercial future. Why does that happen, and what does artificial intelligence change?

By The Legal Fennec Editorial Team  ·  19 October 2026  ·  Approximately 7 min read

Imagine a screenwriter completing a script, a composer delivering a score, or an illustrator designing the visual identity of a film. They are the people who created the work. Yet when that work is released, licensed, adapted or sold, they may have little say in what happens next.

This apparent contradiction lies at the heart of copyright law: the author of a work and the owner of its economic rights need not be the same person. Sometimes they are. Often, they are not. And understanding why tells us something important about the relationship between creativity and commercial power.

Two roles, one work

Authorship asks who created the protected expression. Ownership asks who holds the rights to commercially exploit it, such as reproduction, distribution and licensing. The exact starting rules depend on the country, the type of work and the contractual relationship.

A creator can transfer or license economic rights without ceasing to be the person who authored a work. In many civil-law systems, moral rights, including attribution and protection against certain prejudicial modifications, retain a special connection to that person. Their scope and transferability are not identical everywhere. In the United Kingdom, for example, the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988 contains different rules for employee-created works and provides for waiver of some moral rights.

This distinction matters because recognition is not the same as control. A credit in the film does not necessarily determine who may authorize distribution, negotiate a streaming deal or license an adaptation.

Why producers need rights

It would be misleading to treat every transfer of rights as inherently abusive. A film brings together the work of writers, directors, composers, performers and many other contributors. Financing, insurance, international sales and distribution often depend on an identifiable chain of rights.

If a distributor had to negotiate separately with every contributor whenever a film entered a new market, exploitation could become expensive or impractical. Consolidating appropriate rights can therefore make creative works possible to finance and circulate in the first place.

But that practical need raises a harder question: how should the resulting revenue and control be shared between those who fund a project and those whose creativity gives it value? A contract can enable production while still leaving an individual creator with limited negotiating power.

When bargaining power shapes ownership

Creators may sign agreements at the beginning of their careers, when they need the advance payment, the exposure or simply the opportunity to work. The parties are legally capable of contracting, but they are not necessarily equally capable of negotiating.

Consider a hypothetical independent filmmaker who assigns broad exploitation rights for a one-time payment. Years later, a new distribution format makes the film unexpectedly valuable. Whether the filmmaker receives additional remuneration or recovers any control will depend on the agreement and applicable law, not simply on who originally created the film.

The distinction matters because copyright's familiar justification is that exclusive rights encourage creative activity. If most of the economic benefits flow away from creators, the law may still encourage investment and distribution, but it becomes less clear how well it supports creators' livelihoods over time.

Europe has begun to address the imbalance

The European Union's Directive (EU) 2019/790 on copyright in the Digital Single Market includes contractual protections for authors and performers. Article 18 establishes a principle of appropriate and proportionate remuneration when exclusive rights are licensed or transferred. Article 19 requires certain transparency about the exploitation of works and performances. Article 20 provides a contract-adjustment mechanism in specified circumstances, and Article 22 concerns revocation for lack of exploitation.

These provisions are not a universal guarantee of a particular royalty. Member States implement them through national laws, and the directive includes conditions, exceptions and sector-specific considerations. Still, they acknowledge something basic: a signed contract does not automatically resolve every question of fairness.

And then comes generative AI

AI does not invent the difference between making a work and controlling its economic value. It makes that difference more visible.

In audiovisual production, generative systems can assist with storyboarding, concept art, editing and other creative tasks. Where the resulting material contains substantial AI-generated expression, a new question emerges: which elements, if any, qualify for copyright protection, and whose human choices satisfy the relevant legal test?

In its January 2025 report on copyrightability, the U.S. Copyright Office explained that AI-assisted works can receive protection when sufficient expressive elements are determined by a human author. Merely providing prompts, without more, is not necessarily enough. That is guidance about United States copyright law, not a universal rule for every country.

It is also important not to confuse patents and copyright. The United Kingdom Supreme Court's 2023 Thaler decision addressed whether an AI machine could be named as an inventor under patent law. It did not decide copyright authorship for AI-generated films, texts or images.

There is a further distinction: a company may control access to an AI service, set contractual conditions or possess commercial leverage without necessarily owning a copyright in every output. Practical power, contractual rights and statutory copyright are not interchangeable.

The question behind the question

Copyright has always had to serve more than one purpose. It recognizes creative contribution; it enables investment; it helps work reach audiences. These goals can support each other, but they do not always align.

For filmmakers and other creators, the question is not only Who is credited? It is also: Who can authorize the next use? Who receives revenue? Who sees the accounts? Who can renegotiate when the work succeeds?

Artificial intelligence makes those questions urgent, but it does not replace them. The deeper issue remains whether a system built around authorship can distribute control and value in a way that continues to sustain the people who create.
